COUNTRY-SPECIFIC AI, SURVEILLANCE & DATA PRIVACY POSITIONS
====================================================================

COUNTRY NAME: Russia

1. Country Overview
- Government: Federal Semi-Presidential Republic
- Political System: Centralized system with strong executive power
- Global Influence: Moderate-High (Permanent member of UNSC, BRICS key member, regional military presence)
- Technology Sector: Highly specialized (strong software engineering, cybersecurity, and focus on import substitution)

2. Official Position on AI
Emphasizes digital sovereignty, technological independence, and state security. Views Western regulatory initiatives as tools for geopolitical dominance and advocates for multi-polar digital governance. Rejects external interference in domestic internet regulation and supports state-directed technology integration.

3. National AI Strategy
- National AI plans: Governed by the "National Strategy for the Development of Artificial Intelligence until 2030" (updated 2024).
- Government initiatives: Funding for import-substitution of hardware, development of domestic LLMs, and integration of AI into e-government services.
- AI priorities: Achieving technological autonomy, improving economic efficiency, and enhancing national defense capabilities.

4. AI Governance
- AI regulation: Sectoral approach coordinated by the Ministry of Digital Development and Roskomnadzor.
- AI ethics: Promotes the domestic "AI Ethics Code" signed by leading Russian tech companies, focusing on safety and state security.
- Government oversight: Strong state control over technological infrastructure and software standards.

5. Data Privacy
- Privacy laws: Federal Law No. 152-FZ on Personal Data.
- Data protection authority: Roskomnadzor (Federal Service for Supervision of Communications, Information Technology and Mass Media).
- Citizen protections: Regulates data processing but provides broad access exemptions for national security services.
- Cross-border data rules: Imposes strict data localization mandates, requiring Russian citizens' data to be stored on servers located within Russia.

6. Mass Digital Surveillance
- CCTV: Pervasive camera networks deployed in major metropolitan areas.
- Facial recognition: Deeply integrated into city infrastructure (e.g., Moscow's "Safe City" and "Face Pay" systems).
- Internet monitoring: Broad monitoring of online activity and traffic under SORM (System for Operative-Investigative Activities).
- Government surveillance: FSB has direct physical access to telecom network backbones.
- Public justification: Necessary for counter-terrorism, crime prevention, and public order.
- International criticism: Severely criticized for using facial recognition to track, identify, and arrest political protesters.

7. AI in Public Services
- Education: Integration of AI diagnostics in online learning portals and classroom management.
- Healthcare: AI imaging tools in public diagnostics, medical record management, and drug allocation.
- Transportation: Autonomous metro systems testing, smart traffic optimization, and Face Pay transit ticketing.
- Judiciary: AI used for case document categorization, legal databases, and administrative filings.
- Public administration: Gosuslugi portal automating tax collection, social benefit allocation, and ID distribution.

8. AI in Defence
- Military AI: Strongly integrates AI in electronic warfare, unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), and command systems.
- Autonomous systems: Active testing of autonomous land combat vehicles and surveillance drones.
- Cyber defence: State-led automated threat detection and network perimeter protection.
- National security applications: Communication intercept sorting, automated OSINT gathering, and boundary surveillance.

9. Cybersecurity
- National cyber strategy: Guided by the "Information Security" national program, focusing on import-substitution and the Sovereign Internet.
- Cyber agencies: FSTEK (Federal Service for Technical and Export Control), FSB, and Ministry of Digital Development.
- Cyber threats: State-sponsored espionage, critical infrastructure attacks, and foreign digital blockades.

10. Major Technology Companies
Yandex, Sberbank, Kaspersky Lab, VK Group.

11. International Cooperation
- UN: Active participant in UNGA and UNHRC, promoting resolutions on cybercrime.
- UNESCO: Contributes to ethical discussions on AI.
- OECD: Not a member; opposes Western-dominated OECD frameworks.
- Bilateral agreements: Technology and security agreements with China, Belarus, and Iran.
- Regional initiatives: Technology cooperation within the Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS) and BRICS.

12. Human Rights Strengths
- High domestic capacity for technological self-reliance, reducing dependence on foreign entities.
- Advanced scientific research base and high-quality mathematical education.
- Strong consumer protection guidelines regarding data breaches by private commercial firms.
- Successful digitization of administrative public services, improving accessibility.
- Effective national cybersecurity defense mechanisms protecting public utilities.

13. Human Rights Concerns
- FSB interception of communications under SORM without prior judicial warrants.
- Moscow's facial recognition system used to identify and arrest peaceful protesters.
- Broad internet filtering, blocking of independent news websites, and censorship.
- Data localization laws used to restrict foreign online platforms.
- Lack of independent oversight for Roskomnadzor's blocking decisions.

16. Strong Defence Arguments
- Internet regulations are necessary to defend national sovereignty and counter foreign-funded cyber operations.
- The data localization law protects Russian citizens' personal data from foreign access and surveillance.
- Facial recognition technology in major cities has significantly reduced urban crime rates and helped identify fugitives.
- SORM operations are subject to national legal procedures and are essential for counter-terrorism.
- Russia's focus on technological self-reliance ensures that critical public services remain operational despite foreign sanctions.
- National security measures comply with domestic constitutional requirements and procedures.
- Cyber security strategies protect key national infrastructure from external disruption.
- Russia actively participates in UN HRC discussions on digital rights and privacy.

17. Red Lines
- Will not support any international treaty that limits its sovereign right to control its domestic internet.
- Opposes international regulation of military AI or bans on autonomous weapons.
- Rejects any resolution that criticizes its data localization laws or domestic security surveillance.

18. Likely Allies

19. Likely Opponents

20. Suggested Bloc

21. Negotiation Strategy
Emphasize national sovereignty and the right to self-defense in cyberspace. Align with China to resist Western proposals. Highlight Western double standards on surveillance. Advocate for UN-led, multi-polar digital governance.

22. Important Statistics
Moscow's facial recognition system utilizes over 220,000 cameras, representing one of the largest installations in Europe.

23. Quick Facts
- Federal Law No. 152-FZ requires data to be physically stored inside Russia.
- SORM is the national program for communications interception.
- Yandex is the leading domestic search engine and developer of AI models.
- The Sovereign Internet Law went into effect in 2019.
- FSB has direct physical connections to telecom operator nodes.
- Moscow's metro system uses facial recognition for "Face Pay" ticketing.
- Roskomnadzor has blocked thousands of independent news websites.
- Sberbank has invested heavily in generative AI (GigaChat).
- Kaspersky Lab is a leading global cybersecurity firm based in Moscow.
- Opposes a preemptive ban on lethal autonomous weapons systems.
- Developed the Russian Sovereign Certificate Authority to secure domestic web traffic.
- Holds annual national cybersecurity drills to test internet autonomy.

24. Executive Summary
The Russian Federation advocates for a sovereignty-centric, self-reliant approach to AI and digital governance. Emphasizes the protection of the state from external cyber threats and digital intervention, utilizing data localization laws (152-FZ) and the Sovereign Internet Law (RuNet). Locally, Russia maintains extensive domestic monitoring systems (SORM, Moscow facial recognition) and a strong military AI program, rejecting Western-led international regulations.
