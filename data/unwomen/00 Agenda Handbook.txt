AGENDA: ASSESSING THE IMPACT OF THE GLOBAL CARE ECONOMY ON WOMEN'S ECONOMIC AND SOCIAL MOBILITY
==================================================

Agenda Title: Assessing the Impact of the Global Care Economy on Women's Economic and Social Mobility
Short Form: The Global Care Economy & Women's Mobility
Relevance: Global, Systemic, Macroeconomic & Human Rights

---------------------------------------------------------------
EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
---------------------------------------------------------------
The care economy encompasses all forms of paid and unpaid labor dedicated to the physical, emotional, and developmental care of individuals, including children, the elderly, persons with disabilities, and working adults. Globally, women perform more than 76% of all unpaid care work—totaling an estimated 16.4 billion hours daily, equivalent to 2 billion people working full-time without pay. If monetized, this invisible labor represents at least $11 trillion to $14 trillion annually, or approximately 9% to 12% of global Gross Domestic Product (GDP).

Despite being the indispensable foundation that makes all other economic productivity possible, the care economy functions as a major structural barrier to women's economic independence and social mobility. Women bear the brunt of "time poverty," which limits their labor force participation, restricts access to education, forces reliance on precarious informal employment, suppresses lifelong wage growth, and creates massive retirement pension deficits.

Furthermore, increasing demand for care in high-income, aging economies has generated "global care chains"—the transnational movement of female care and domestic workers from developing countries (such as the Philippines, Indonesia, and South Asia) to affluent households in North America, Europe, the Middle East, and East Asia. While this migration generates vital remittances, it frequently exposes migrant women to severe vulnerabilities, lack of legal protection, family separation, and a domestic "care drain" in their countries of origin.

---------------------------------------------------------------
CORE DIMENSIONS OF THE CARE ECONOMY
---------------------------------------------------------------
1. UNPAID CARE WORK
• Direct care: Looking after children, nursing sick family members, providing eldercare.
• Indirect care: Meal preparation, water and fuel collection, cleaning, laundry, household maintenance.
• Systemic penalty: Time spent on unpaid care directly suppresses hours available for paid employment, political participation, and rest.

2. PAID CARE WORK
• Education, healthcare, childcare, eldercare, nursing, and private domestic service.
• Characterized globally by structural underpayment, lack of collective bargaining, occupational segregation, and informality.
• Over 75.6 million domestic workers worldwide (over 76% female), with more than 80% working in informal arrangements without social security.

3. GLOBAL CARE CHAINS & TRANSNATIONAL MIGRATION
• Flow of care labor from the Global South to the Global North and Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) states.
• Emotional surplus transfer: Migrant mothers caring for children abroad while leaving their own children in the care of relatives at home.
• Exploitative labor frameworks: Kafala sponsorship systems, tied visas, exclusion from national labor codes, and lack of portability for social security benefits.

4. ECONOMIC & SOCIAL MOBILITY IMPACTS
• Labor Force Participation: Global female labor force participation stands at approximately 47%, compared to 72% for men—a gap primarily driven by unpaid caregiving responsibilities.
• The "Motherhood Penalty": Women experience sharp declines in earnings, promotion rates, and career progression following childbirth, while fathers frequently experience a "fatherhood premium."
• Lifetime Pension Gap: Lower lifetime earnings and career interruptions translate into women receiving 26% to 40% lower retirement pensions than men globally, exacerbating elderly poverty.

---------------------------------------------------------------
KEY INTERNATIONAL LEGAL FRAMEWORKS
---------------------------------------------------------------
• UN Charter: Article 55 (promoting higher standards of living, full employment, and conditions of economic and social progress without distinction as to sex).
• Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR): Article 22 (right to social security), Article 23 (right to desirable work and equal remuneration), Article 25 (right to an adequate standard of living, mother and child care).
• CEDAW: Article 11 (elimination of discrimination in employment and social protection); General Recommendation 17 (measurement and valuation of unremunerated domestic activities); General Recommendation 26 (women migrant workers).
• Beijing Declaration & Platform for Action (1995): Strategic Objective F (Women and the Economy)—recognizing, reducing, and redistributing unpaid work.
• 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development:
  - SDG Target 5.4: "Recognize and value unpaid care and domestic work through the provision of public services, infrastructure and social protection policies and the promotion of shared responsibility within the household and the family."
  - SDG Target 8.5: Decent work and equal pay for work of equal value.
  - SDG Target 8.8: Protect labor rights and safe working environments for all workers, including migrant workers.
  - SDG Target 10.4: Adopt fiscal, wage, and social protection policies to achieve greater equality.
• International Labour Organization (ILO) Standards:
  - ILO Convention No. 189 (Domestic Workers Convention, 2011): Decent working conditions, written contracts, minimum wage, and rest periods for domestic workers.
  - ILO Convention No. 156 (Workers with Family Responsibilities Convention, 1981): Equal opportunity without discrimination based on family obligations.
  - ILO Convention No. 100 (Equal Remuneration Convention, 1951).
  - ILO Convention No. 102 (Social Security Minimum Standards Convention, 1952).

---------------------------------------------------------------
THE "5R" POLICY FRAMEWORK FOR THE CARE ECONOMY
---------------------------------------------------------------
The recognized multilateral roadmap endorsed by UN Women and the ILO is the 5R Framework:
1. RECOGNIZE: Measure unpaid care work through national Time-Use Surveys and integrate care into National Accounts (satellite accounts).
2. REDUCE: Lessen time spent on arduous domestic labor through investments in public infrastructure (clean water, electricity, sanitation, public transport).
3. REDISTRIBUTE: Share care responsibilities more equitably between women and men, and between families, the state, private sector, and communities (paternity leave, public childcare).
4. REWARD: Ensure decent work, living wages, social security coverage, and equal pay for equal work for paid care and domestic workers.
5. REPRESENT: Guarantee freedom of association, collective bargaining, and representation in social dialogue and policy-making for care workers.

---------------------------------------------------------------
BLOC DYNAMICS & DEBATE CLEAVAGES
---------------------------------------------------------------
• Developed / Care-Importing Nations (USA, UK, Western Europe, Japan, South Korea):
  - Strengths: High state investment in childcare subsidies and formal eldercare systems.
  - Vulnerabilities: Heavy reliance on low-wage migrant labor to fill domestic eldercare shortages; high childcare costs; significant private-sector gender wage gaps; aging demographics straining pension systems.
• Developing / Care-Exporting Nations (Philippines, Indonesia, Bangladesh, South Asia):
  - Strengths: Comprehensive progressive legal frameworks (e.g., Philippines' Batas Kasambahay RA 10361, Magna Carta of Women RA 9710), proactive diaspora protection laws, strong advocacy for ILO C189.
  - Vulnerabilities: Significant domestic unpaid care burden; lower female labor force participation; remittance dependency; domestic "care drain" in health and domestic sectors; vulnerabilities of citizens under foreign kafala schemes.
• Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) Nations (UAE, Saudi Arabia):
  - Strengths: Growing female education and public sector workforce participation under modern reform agendas.
  - Vulnerabilities: Domestic reliance on foreign domestic labor under sponsorship systems; gaps in labor law coverage for private household workers; enforcement of standard employment contracts.
• Transition & Emerging Economies (Brazil, South Africa, Turkey, Russia, India):
  - Diverse domestic care realities; high informal economy proportions; acute regional divides between urban formal care and rural unpaid burden.

---------------------------------------------------------------
DELEGATE STRATEGY FOR THE COMMITTEE
---------------------------------------------------------------
• Focus debate on practical, institutional mechanisms rather than abstract assertions.
• Champions of care reform must connect unpaid care work directly to macroeconomic productivity: investing 2% of GDP in care infrastructure creates 2.5 times more jobs than equivalent spending in construction, 70% of which go to women.
• Address both ends of the migration chain: host countries must provide legal equality, portability of social security, and labor inspection access, while sending countries must build domestic employment alternatives and pre-departure protections.
• Maintain diplomatic decorum while persistently highlighting implementation gaps between statutory ratification and lived reality.
