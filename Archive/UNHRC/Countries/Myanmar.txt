COUNTRY-SPECIFIC AI, SURVEILLANCE & DATA PRIVACY POSITIONS
====================================================================

COUNTRY NAME: Myanmar

1. Country Overview
- Government: Military Administration
- Political System: Governed by the State Administration Council (SAC) under a state of emergency
- Global Influence: Low (Subject to international sanctions, partner in regional networks)
- Technology Sector: Restricted (dependent on foreign telecommunications hardware and software)

2. Official Position on AI
Prioritizes national security, counter-insurgency, and maintaining public order. Rejects international criticism of its telecommunications monitoring, stating it is a necessary sovereign response to domestic armed conflict and terrorism. Advocates for sovereign control over network boundaries.

3. National AI Strategy
- National AI plans: No formal national AI strategy due to conflict.
- Government initiatives: Focuses on telecommunications control and urban surveillance.
- AI priorities: Public security, crowd monitoring, and biometric registration.

4. AI Governance
- AI regulation: Governed primarily by the Ministry of Transport and Communications (MoTC).
- AI ethics: None.
- Government oversight: Managed directly through military decrees and communications directives.

5. Data Privacy
- Privacy laws: Basic privacy protections in the constitution were suspended or amended following the 2021 coup.
- Data protection authority: Governed provisionally by the MoTC.
- Citizen protections: Extremely weak; state agencies have broad powers to search, seize, and monitor digital communications.
- Cross-border data rules: Draft Cybersecurity Law proposes strict data localization mandates.

6. Mass Digital Surveillance
- CCTV: Deployed extensively in Naypyidaw, Yangon, and Mandalay.
- Facial recognition: Integrated into urban CCTV cameras, sourced from foreign suppliers.
- Internet monitoring: Monitors telecommunications traffic and uses Deep Packet Inspection (DPI) to block VPNs.
- Government surveillance: Security forces monitor mobile networks under emergency laws.
- Public justification: Critical for maintaining public order and preventing counter-state terrorism.
- International criticism: Criticized by CSOs and the UN for using facial recognition to track anti-coup activists.

7. AI in Public Services
- Education: Limited basic digital learning portals.
- Healthcare: Basic public health database support in state hospitals.
- Transportation: Camera-based traffic security tracking.
- Judiciary: Automated document indexing in state tribunals.
- Public administration: Mandatory biometric SIM card registration databases.

8. AI in Defence
- Military AI: Limited domestic capacity; uses imported drone and reconnaissance systems.
- Autonomous systems: Unmanned aerial vehicles used for border surveillance.
- Cyber defence: Focused on securing military communications and state databases.
- National security applications: Bulk signal monitoring and communication filtering.

9. Cybersecurity
- National cyber strategy: Guided by draft cybersecurity decrees focusing on network security.
- Cyber agencies: Ministry of Transport and Communications.
- Cyber threats: Opposition cyber groups, database breaches, and state-sponsored espionage.

10. Major Technology Companies
Not applicable.

11. International Cooperation
- UN: Represented under contested status; participates in regional tracks.
- UNESCO: Participates in cultural preservation tracks.
- OECD: Not a member.
- Bilateral agreements: Sourced technology and surveillance cooperation with Russia and China.
- Regional initiatives: Participates in regional telecom discussions.

12. Human Rights Strengths
- Centralized network controls, enabling rapid detection of cybercrime and security threats.
- High mobile phone penetration, facilitating basic communication channels.
- Implementation of structured SIM registration to reduce fraud.
- State-backed efforts to maintain basic telecommunication services during conflict.
- Digital literacy initiatives in state training centers.

13. Human Rights Concerns
- Suspension of basic constitutional privacy protections.
- Criminalization of the use of security tools like VPNs.
- Deployment of facial recognition cameras to target peaceful protesters.
- Extensive localized internet shutdowns in conflict zones.
- Unrestricted access by military security to private communication data.

14. Likely Questions Against This Country
- How does the military administration defend the deployment of facial recognition cameras to track and arrest peaceful protesters?
- Why has the administration suspended citizens' privacy protections and criminalized the use of security tools like VPNs?
- How do localized internet shutdowns in conflict zones align with international human rights?
- What measures protect citizen data from unauthorized access by military personnel?
- How does the draft Cybersecurity Law protect freedom of expression?
- What checks prevent biometric SIM card databases from being used for political targeting?
- How is the import of surveillance technology from overseas suppliers regulated?
- What oversight exists to monitor the blocking of independent news portals?
- Does the military's use of drones in conflict zones comply with the Geneva Conventions?
- Why are there no independent privacy watchdogs in Myanmar?

15. Questions Myanmar Can Ask Others
- How can sovereign states protect their digital space from foreign-funded campaigns that encourage violent insurrection?
- What measures should prevent foreign platforms from harboring channels that promote domestic instability?
- How do states secure utility networks from state-sponsored cyber operations?
- Why should the UN not respect national sovereignty and cyber boundaries?
- What actions protect developing states from digital intervention by global tech monopolies?

16. Strong Defence Arguments
- Digital restrictions, telecommunications tracking, and security cameras are necessary to protect national sovereignty and counter domestic armed terrorism.
- The administration has the duty to maintain public order and protect critical infrastructure during a state of emergency.
- Biometric SIM card registration prevents telecommunications fraud and secures identity records.
- The state is developing domestic cyber capacities to protect public services from hostile foreign cyber attacks.
- Internet suspensions are temporary, targeting specific conflict zones to prevent coordination of armed attacks.
- Domestic security operations are conducted within the legal framework of the state of emergency.

17. Red Lines
- Will not support international regulations that restrict national security operations or internet gateways.
- Rejects any resolution that singles out Myanmar's military administration for criticism.

18. Likely Allies
Russia, China.

19. Likely Opponents
United States, United Kingdom, France, Japan, Australia, ASEAN Member States.

20. Suggested Bloc
Sovereign Bloc / Russia-China aligned.

21. Negotiation Strategy
Emphasize national security, counter-terrorism, and cyber sovereignty. Counter Western proposals as political interventions. Align with Russia and China.

22. Important Statistics
Since 2021, the military administration has ordered hundreds of localized internet blackouts and blocked major global social media networks.

23. Quick Facts
- MoTC leads national telecom policy.
- Draft Cybersecurity Law criminalizes VPN use.
- Facial recognition deployed in Naypyidaw and Yangon.
- Biometric SIM card registration is mandatory.
- Internet blackouts are frequently used in active conflict zones.
- Major global platforms (Facebook, Twitter) are blocked.
- Deep Packet Inspection (DPI) technology is utilized.
- Tech import relies on regional suppliers.
- Lacks independent data privacy watchdogs.
- Governed under State Administration Council decrees.
- Internet penetration is estimated at around 45% nationally.
- Subject to extensive international technology export restrictions.

24. Executive Summary
Myanmar's military administration prioritizes national security, counter-insurgency, and public order. Rejects international criticism of its digital surveillance and internet censorship, claiming them as necessary measures against domestic terrorism.
